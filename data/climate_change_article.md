# Global Climate Change: State of Play, 2026

*Format: Markdown (.md) — Category: Explainer / News-style Article*

## Introduction

2024 was confirmed by the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) as
the warmest year on record, with global average surface temperature
about **1.55°C ± 0.13°C above the 1850–1900 pre-industrial baseline** —
likely the first calendar year to top the Paris Agreement's 1.5°C
reference point. The ten years from 2015–2024 were, collectively, the
warmest decade ever measured. This article summarizes the physical
science, the numbers behind global emissions, the policy response
through COP30, and what's being done about it.

## The Greenhouse Effect, Briefly

1. Sunlight warms Earth's surface.
2. The surface radiates heat back toward space as infrared radiation.
3. Greenhouse gases (GHGs) — mainly CO₂, methane, and nitrous oxide —
   absorb part of that outgoing heat and re-radiate it, warming the
   lower atmosphere.
4. Human emissions have sharply increased GHG concentrations since the
   mid-1700s, intensifying a process that is otherwise essential for a
   habitable planet.

## CO₂: The Headline Number

| Milestone | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-industrial CO₂ (~1750) | ~280 ppm | Ice core records |
| 2024 annual global average | 422.8 ppm | NOAA Global Monitoring Lab |
| 2024→2025 largest one-year jump | +3.75 ppm | NOAA |
| 2025 annual global average | 427.5 ppm (record) | NOAA/Statista |
| May 2025 seasonal peak | 430.5 ppm (first time over 430) | NOAA / Scripps |
| May 2026 seasonal peak | ~434 ppm (new record) | Scripps/NOAA Mauna Loa |

CO₂ is now roughly **50% higher** than pre-industrial levels, and — based
on ice-core evidence — higher than at any point in at least the past
800,000 years. Every single year since continuous monitoring began in
1959 has recorded a higher concentration than the year before.

## Who Emits the Most? (2024 data)

Global GHG emissions reached a record **53.2 billion tonnes of CO₂-
equivalent (GtCO₂e)** in 2024 (up 1.3% from 2023), according to the
European Commission's EDGAR database. Breakdown by gas: fossil CO₂
(~74.5%), methane (~17.9%), nitrous oxide (~4.8%), fluorinated gases
(~2.8%).

| Emitter | Approx. share of global GHG emissions (2024) | Trend vs. 2023 | Per-capita emissions |
|---|---|---|---|
| China | ~29–32% | +0.8% to +0.2% | ~9–11 tCO₂e |
| United States | ~11–14% | +0.4% to −0.6% | ~17–18 tCO₂e |
| India | ~7–8% | +3.9% to +4.6% (fastest-growing major emitter) | ~2–3 tCO₂e |
| European Union (27) | ~6–7% | −1.8% to −3.8% (only major bloc trending down) | ~5.6 tCO₂e |
| Russia | — | +2.4% | ~14.1 tCO₂e |
| Indonesia | — | +5.0% (largest relative increase) | — |
| Brazil | — | +0.2% | — |
| Japan | — | −2.8% | — |

The top eight emitters above account for roughly **61–68% of global
emissions** while holding about 55% of world population and 68% of
global GDP. The **G20** collectively accounts for about **83%** of
global CO₂ emissions. On a per-person basis, Saudi Arabia (~17.7 tCO₂),
Canada (~14.9), Australia (~14.4), and Russia (~14.1) rank highest
among large economies — notably higher than China's per-capita figure
despite China's larger total.

## Observed Impacts

- **Sea level rise:** ~3.3–4.5 mm/year since satellite altimetry began
  in 1993, and accelerating.
- **Arctic sea ice:** September minimum extent now typically 3.9–4.5
  million km², down from ~7.5–8 million km² in the early 1980s; the
  lowest on record was 2012.
- **Ocean impacts:** oceans have absorbed over 90% of excess heat from
  the enhanced greenhouse effect and roughly a quarter of human CO₂
  emissions, driving measurable acidification.
- **Extreme weather (illustrative recent events):**
  - 2019–2020 Australian "Black Summer" bushfires — 18–24 million
    hectares burned.
  - 2021 Pacific Northwest heat dome — temperatures topped 45–49°C in
    normally temperate parts of the US and Canada.
  - 2022 Pakistan floods — tens of millions displaced, tens of billions
    of dollars in damage, linked to an intensified monsoon.
  - 2023 Canadian wildfires — worst season on record by area burned
    (over 15 million hectares).
  - 2024 — warmest year on record, with national heat records broken
    across Asia, Europe, the Middle East, and the Americas.

## The Policy Timeline, Through COP30

| Year | Milestone |
|---|---|
| 1988 | IPCC established by WMO and UNEP |
| 1992 | UNFCCC adopted at the Rio Earth Summit |
| 1997 | Kyoto Protocol — first binding targets, developed nations |
| 2015 | **Paris Agreement** (COP21) — "well below 2°C," pursue 1.5°C |
| 2021 | Glasgow Climate Pact (COP26) — first COP text on phasing down coal |
| 2023 | COP28, Dubai — first UNFCCC Global Stocktake concludes; first COP decision language on "transitioning away from fossil fuels" |
| 2024 | COP29, Baku — New Collective Quantified Goal (NCQG) on climate finance |
| 2025 | **COP30, Belem, Brazil** (Nov 10–22) — the "Belem Package" adopted by 195 Parties |

### What COP30 actually delivered

Held for the first time on the edge of the Amazon rainforest, COP30 was
billed as the "implementation COP." Its outcomes (the "global mutirão,"
a Portuguese/Tupi-Guarani term for collective community effort):

- A goal to **triple global adaptation finance by 2035** (the Africa
  Group and others had pushed for 2030 instead).
- A new **Just Transition Mechanism**.
- A **Global Implementation Accelerator** and the voluntary **"Belem
  Mission to 1.5"** initiative to keep the 1.5°C goal within reach.
- A Brazilian-led **tropical forest fund** that raised about **$6.6
  billion** at launch, more than half from Norway and Germany.
- **122+ countries** submitted new or updated Nationally Determined
  Contributions (NDCs).
- A proposed binding roadmap to phase out fossil fuels — pushed by the
  EU and others — was **not adopted**; roughly 84 countries opposed
  including it in the core decision text.
- Technical items — Global Stocktake tracking and new 2035 NDC
  trajectories — were **deferred to Bonn intersessional talks in 2026**.

## The Energy Transition, By the Numbers (2024)

- Global renewable power capacity grew by a **record 582–585 GW**
  (+15.1% year-on-year) — the fastest annual growth rate on record —
  bringing total installed renewable capacity to about **4,448 GW**,
  roughly **46% of global power capacity**.
- **Solar PV** alone added about 452–453 GW (the majority of it in
  China, which installed nearly 64% of the world's new renewable
  capacity in 2024). **Wind** added about 113–114 GW. Solar and wind
  together made up **96–97%** of net renewable growth.
- Despite the record, IRENA estimates the world remains short of the
  **11.2 TW** needed by 2030 to meet the COP28 pledge to triple global
  renewable capacity — requiring **16.6% annual growth** through 2030.
- IEA: renewables were the largest source of global energy supply
  growth in 2024 (~38%), ahead of natural gas (28%), coal (15%), oil
  (11%), and nuclear (8%).

## Mitigation vs. Adaptation

| Mitigation (cut emissions) | Adaptation (manage impacts) |
|---|---|
| Renewable energy build-out | Climate-resilient infrastructure |
| Electrification of transport/heating | Drought/flood-resistant agriculture |
| Energy efficiency | Early warning systems |
| Carbon pricing (taxes, ETS) | Coastal defenses, mangrove restoration |
| Forest protection & restoration | Water resource management |
| Methane leak reduction | Adaptation finance (tripling pledged by 2035 at COP30) |
| Carbon capture & storage (CCUS) | Managed retreat where necessary |

## Why This Matters for Risk and Finance

Financial institutions increasingly assess:
- **Physical risk** — direct damage to assets and operations from
  extreme weather and chronic shifts (heat stress, sea level rise).
- **Transition risk** — exposure to policy, carbon pricing, and
  technology shifts as economies decarbonize.

Disclosure expectations building on the former Task Force on Climate-
related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) framework — now consolidated into
the IFRS Sustainability Disclosure Standards — are increasingly used by
regulators and central banks in stress testing and scenario analysis.

## Conclusion

The physical trend lines — CO₂, temperature, sea level, ice extent —
have moved in one direction for decades, even as the policy and energy
landscape shifts rapidly: record renewable buildout, a still-widening
gap to 2030 targets, and incremental but contested progress at COP30.
Addressing climate change remains a systemic challenge spanning energy,
agriculture, finance, and urban planning.
